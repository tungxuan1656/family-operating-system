# User Journey & Key Scenarios

## Document Purpose

This document translates the product concept into a practical experience flow.
It captures the household journey from initial setup to repeated use, including
emotional tension points that product and engineering must preserve in later
design.

## End-To-End Journey

### Stage 1: Trigger

A parent feels the current home dynamic is unfair, invisible, or difficult to
discuss. They begin searching for a tool that can make family contribution more
visible without creating more conflict.

Desired product outcome:

- the product immediately communicates fairness, recognition, and playfulness;
- the first impression feels safe, not judgmental.

### Stage 2: Household Setup

The first user creates a family space, adds members, and explains the concept to
others.

Desired product outcome:

- setup feels lightweight;
- the rules are easy to explain;
- the household understands this is a shared support tool, not a punishment
  system.

### Stage 3: First Contributions

Users start recording chores, support actions, or appreciation moments. The
household sees points or recognition appear in a shared timeline.

Desired product outcome:

- initial actions feel rewarding quickly;
- the first session proves visible value;
- there is no confusion about what happened and why.

### Stage 4: Reward And Request Use

A member uses accumulated points to request or redeem a reward. Another member
accepts, delays, or rejects the request.

Desired product outcome:

- requests feel respectful and voluntary;
- reward exchange feels meaningful rather than transactional;
- rejection does not break trust in the system.

### Stage 5: Habit Formation

The family starts using the product repeatedly, especially around recurring
chores, child contribution, and appreciation habits.

Desired product outcome:

- repeat usage becomes easy and low-friction;
- the product reinforces healthy routines;
- the system surfaces progress without demanding constant maintenance.

### Stage 6: Reflection And Rebalancing

Family members use their history and contribution patterns to discuss fairness
and adjust behavior.

Desired product outcome:

- the product acts as a conversation aid;
- insight creates correction without blame;
- members remain willing to keep participating.

## Key Scenario 1: Record A Household Contribution

Primary actor: parent or partner

Base flow:

1. User records a completed contribution.
2. User assigns a contribution type and point value.
3. If approval is needed, another adult reviews it.
4. The action appears in family history.
5. Points are updated after approval.

Happy path:

- contribution is easy to add;
- approval is fast;
- everyone understands why points changed.

Hesitation path:

- user is unsure whether a task deserves points;
- the system needs lightweight guidance instead of complex rules.

Conflict path:

- another adult disputes the value or fairness of the contribution;
- the product must avoid escalation and support respectful review.

## Key Scenario 2: Record Emotional Care Or Appreciation

Primary actor: parent or partner

Base flow:

1. User records an action such as helping emotionally, planning a family moment,
   or doing something thoughtful.
2. The action is visible in the family timeline.
3. The action may earn Love Points, which are the only non-Task point unit used
   in MVP.

Happy path:

- the act feels warm and appreciated;
- recognition is visible even without a reward exchange.

Hesitation path:

- users may wonder whether emotional care should be measured at all.

Conflict path:

- if emotional care becomes over-scored or too mechanical, users may reject the
  product premise entirely.

## Key Scenario 3: Create And Redeem A Reward

Primary actor: any family member

Base flow:

1. User creates a reward.
2. The reward is priced in points.
3. Another family member redeems or requests it.
4. The reward owner chooses accept, delay, or reject.
5. The outcome is recorded clearly.

Happy path:

- reward feels motivating and emotionally meaningful.

Hesitation path:

- users may question whether some rewards are appropriate to "buy" with points.

Conflict path:

- requests may feel demanding if the product frames them too rigidly or too
  publicly.

## Key Scenario 4: Parents Recognize A Child Contribution

Primary actor: child, with parent mediation

Base flow:

1. Child completes a task.
2. Parent verifies completion.
3. Parent grants points and recognition.
4. Child sees progress toward a reward.

Happy path:

- rules feel consistent;
- the child sees a direct link between action and outcome.

Hesitation path:

- parents may disagree about how much a task is worth.

Conflict path:

- if reward logic changes unpredictably, the child sees the system as unfair and
  loses trust.

## Key Scenario 5: Resolve A Fairness Tension

Primary actor: one or both adults

Base flow:

1. A user notices an imbalance or feels unseen.
2. They look at household history and trends.
3. They use the information to discuss patterns.
4. The family adjusts behavior, rules, or expectations.

Happy path:

- the product helps the conversation stay factual and calm.

Hesitation path:

- users may avoid opening the discussion if they fear defensiveness.

Conflict path:

- if the product appears to take sides, it may intensify blame instead of
  helping.

## Journey Design Requirements

The journey should preserve these product truths:

- onboarding must explain the social contract clearly;
- the first contribution must deliver visible value fast;
- rewards/requests must remain voluntary and respectful;
- children need consistency more than complexity;
- fairness insight must support discussion, not accusation.
